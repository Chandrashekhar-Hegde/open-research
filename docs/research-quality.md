# Research frustrations, evidence and concrete responses

The frustration is work that looks finished before the evidence is inspectable.
A journal label, preprint status, polished language or completed checklist cannot
by itself establish that a study was conducted well. Equally, lack of new data
collection is not evidence of misconduct: reviews, reanalyses and proofs can be
legitimate research. Assess the actual claim and method.

## What we want to make harder

| Frustration / failure mode | What to inspect | Workbench response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A conclusion with no executed study behind it | Original observations or source corpus, procedure and outputs | Keep planned methods separate from observed results; missing results stay blank |
| A review that selects only convenient papers | Search dates/queries, eligibility, excluded records, bias appraisal | Search/evidence ledgers and explicit synthesis method |
| Hypotheses or outcomes changed after results are known | Dated protocol, analysis plan and changes | Preserve versions and label exploratory decisions |
| A sophisticated analysis that cannot answer its question | Units, comparison, identification assumptions, sampling and measurement | Method-specific design decisions before analysis |
| Numerical results without reproducible inputs | Data version, dictionary, command, environment and output | Local records, source hashes and executable reanalysis |
| Citations that do not support the sentence | Actual source, exact page/table/section and contradictory evidence | Claims linked to inspected source IDs; never generate references as facts |
| Null findings or inconvenient cases disappearing | Planned outcomes, exclusions and all findings | Record negative results, deviations and unresolved contradictions |
| Publication treated as a substitute for scrutiny | Methods, corrections, data/code access and actual review scope | A review record stating who checked what; self-review stays labeled |
| Institutional pressure rewarding quantity over reliability | Appropriate empirical evidence about incentives | Make reusable records easier; do not claim this tool repairs institutional incentives |

These are failure modes to check, not allegations against every published paper.
When criticizing a particular study, cite the precise passage, inspect corrections
and allow alternative explanations. A missing description may mean incomplete
reporting; it does not prove fabrication or reveal an editor's motives.

## What the evidence establishes

A 2024 international survey of biomedical researchers obtained 1,630 usable
responses (7% response rate). Of respondents, 62% identified publication pressure
as always or very often contributing to irreproducibility. These are reported
perceptions, with response and sampling limitations; they are not a measured
causal explanation for any individual journal decision.
[Cobey et al., abstract, methods and limitations](https://journals.plos.org/plosbiology/article?id=10.1371/journal.pbio.3002870).

[STROBE's aims](https://www.strobe-statement.org/) explain why inadequate reporting
makes assessment difficult and distinguish reporting guidance from study-quality
assessment. [Cochrane](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-01)
and [PRISMA](https://www.prisma-statement.org/prisma-2020) offer a more inspectable
route for reviews than an undocumented collection of citations.
[COS preregistration guidance](https://www.cos.io/initiatives/prereg) distinguishes
planned and exploratory work and explains how to disclose changes.

These sources support transparency practices. They do not establish that this
workbench improves research quality, eliminates fabricated papers, or reliably
detects misconduct. See the [community evaluation plan](community-needs.md) for
what still needs to be measured with consenting users.

## How to review a report here

1. State the claim and the design needed to support it.
2. Trace it to a source row, inspected passage or computed output.
3. Check what was actually done against the protocol and disclosed changes.
4. Rerun the computation where possible; examine bias, uncertainty and alternatives.
5. Record a concrete correction, an unresolved question, or the scope of agreement.

Use respectful, specific feedback. Publish permitted review records and failed
checks as well as successes. Do not substitute a score or an accusation for evidence.
