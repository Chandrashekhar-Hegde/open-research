ABOUT ME · CHANDRASHEKHAR HEGDE

Why I built Open Research

This began with the difficulties I was facing in my own research. I wanted a way to make the work more understandable and less frustrating for myself.

The frustration behind it

Finding and sourcing papers took effort. Reading them took more. I still had to understand the question, work out what the authors actually did, follow their sources, and decide whether the evidence was useful for my own research. Sometimes I reached the end of an entire paper before realizing that it had not been worth the time I had put into it. That was difficult to keep dealing with.

Some papers did not help answer the question I was working on. Others left me unconvinced by the methods or the connection between their evidence and conclusions. Those are different problems, but both made it harder to know what to read next, what to trust, and how to move my own study forward.

That experience also shaped my frustration with research and review writing. I want to understand how a study was done, why particular papers were included in a review, and what actually supports a conclusion. Polished writing and a long reference list leave me frustrated when I still cannot follow the reasoning or inspect the work behind them.

What I am trying to change in my own work

I built Open Research mainly for myself: to make my question explicit, choose a suitable study design, keep track of sources while reading, and connect my analysis to the claims I eventually write. I want to notice gaps earlier, record why a paper is useful or unsuitable, and come back to the work without having to reconstruct every decision.

I also want a clearer way to use modern AI tools in that process. Faster writing does not resolve my need to understand the research. I want assistance that stays connected to the actual question, checked sources, stated assumptions and calculations I can inspect. When something has not been read, verified or executed, I want that to remain visible.

Why I made it open

This is a personal attempt to build a research workflow I can rely on and keep improving. I made it public because other people may recognize the same frustrations and find parts of it useful. They can copy it, adapt it, question it and help correct it.

My motivation is personal; whether the tool helps others still needs to be tested. I want to spend more of my effort understanding a problem and doing the study, with a clear account of what I know, what I checked and what remains unresolved.

PURPOSE, EVIDENCE & LIMITS

Purpose, evidence and limits

Modern AI tools can make it easy to produce an answer before understanding the problem. Open Research exists to help people preserve decisions, inspect evidence, and use computation responsibly. The public benefit we aim for is research that others can challenge and reuse, including inconclusive work.

We measure success by useful, inspectable work and fewer avoidable obstacles. Paper counts, model agreement, and completed checkboxes are not evidence of scientific quality. This project does not automate peer review or journal submission.

Published community feedback

People need control and verification

A 2025 study of social scientists used 284 surveys and 15 interviews. It reports concerns about automation bias and the importance of human verification. Its US university sample and low response rate limit generalization.

What belongs here, and what does not?

Keep portable plans, traceable evidence, understandable calculations and short records that help another person resume the work. Skip process that adds no useful artifact. Use established tools such as Jupyter, Zotero, Quarto, SymPy and Gambit where they already work.

No automatic scientific-validity score, invented participants, paper factory, or promise to fix institutional research incentives. AI can assist; accountable human reasoning and appropriate review remain essential.

This workbench is intended for researchers, students and small teams comfortable with local files. If your existing workflow works well, take only the useful pieces.

WHAT FRUSTRATES US

Finished-looking papers. Unfinished evidence.

The problem is a gap between a claim and the work needed to support it. Reviews, reanalyses and proofs can be real research without new participants. What matters is whether the stated method was carried out and whether its evidence supports the conclusion.

  • Conclusions before execution. Keep a protocol separate from observed results. If nothing was measured or computed, say so.
  • Reviews with an invisible selection process. Record searches, eligibility, exclusions, extraction and bias appraisal.
  • Methods chosen to fit a favorable result. Date plans and changes; label exploration honestly.
  • Numbers nobody can reproduce. Preserve input versions, units, code, commands and failed checks.
  • Citations that do not support the claim. Inspect the source and exact passage; record contradictory evidence.
  • Publication mistaken for validation. Inspect what reviewers actually checked. Journal acceptance and preprint status do not replace methodological scrutiny.

A 2024 biomedical survey received 1,630 usable responses, a 7% response rate. Publication pressure was a commonly perceived contributor to irreproducibility. This describes respondents' perceptions, not proof of any author's or editor's motives. Read the original survey and its limits.

We respond with inspectable artifacts, method guidance and reproducible code. We do not claim a checklist detects misconduct or that this workbench has solved institutional incentives. Read the evidence and practical review procedure →

How we will test usefulness

The proposed first step is 6–8 voluntary usability sessions using comparable practice tasks, alternating usual workflow and Open Research. Record completion, time including setup, assistance, unsupported claims, and frustration. This is a formative pilot, not a powered effectiveness study; it has not been conducted.

Observe whether the stage-specific checks and saved study reduce confusion. Publish permitted aggregate findings, including failures. Fix reproducible defects; simplify steps that repeatedly obstruct users. If the workbench adds no useful artifact, reduce it to the examples people actually use.
